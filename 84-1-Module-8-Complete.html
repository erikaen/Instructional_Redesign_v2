What you earned

Signs became sides

The sign problem makes one symbol do too much: a plus or minus can describe either an amount or a direction of change. The accountant trades signs for sides. Debit means left; credit means right. Each account has a home side, and that home side carries the direction.

The complete event

The journal entry is the accountant’s sentence

A journal entry holds a complex event whole: every account affected, every side used, and every amount that moves. It brings its own self-check—debits equal credits—and its own discipline: a plug is something to explain, not a substitute for reasoning through the event.

The account view

The T-account turns the sentence into a picture

A T-account is the picture of one account: left and right, beginning amount, changes, and ending amount. For an analyst, it is also a solve-for-the-missing-number device. When three parts are known, the account’s structure lets you reason backward to the fourth.

Underneath the statements

Choose the view that fits the question

The sign problem is why sides beat signs: debit means left, and credit means right. Journal entries give events spanning accounts a clear grammar. A T-account pictures one account and is the go-to tool for analyzing its knowns and unknowns. The general ledger is the book of all the accounts. One account → the T-account; events spanning accounts → journal-entry notation.
